LONGEVITY SCENARIOS

What changes if retirement lasts ten years longer?

Planning for a single age can hide the part of retirement that matters most: the years beyond the first estimate. A range of horizons is more useful than a prediction of your lifespan.

Why use several horizons instead of one average? See the OECD life-expectancy comparison for Japan and other countries.

Keep one assumption fixed, then change the time

Suppose your essential monthly spending exceeds reliable monthly income by 500 units of your currency. The table holds that gap constant to isolate the effect of time. It is deliberately simple: no investment growth, inflation, tax change or additional spending is included.

The extra ten years in this example add 60,000 units. This is a difference between hypothetical totals, not a required balance at retirement or a safe withdrawal calculation. Change the inputs yourself before comparing alternatives.

Which assumption moves this example most?

Here is a second, reproducible worksheet using the guide's illustrative monthly spending of 2,500 and reliable income of 2,000. The baseline gap is 500. We change spending, income and years explicitly, with no claim that any change is likely. All totals equal (monthly spending − monthly income) × 12 × years, in arbitrary currency units. There is no return, inflation adjustment or discount rate.

Original sensitivity calculation for an invented household

Ten more years2,500 / 2,00050030180,000

Income 10% lower2,500 / 1,80070030252,000
Both changes2,750 / 1,80095030342,000

The extra decade alone adds 60,000 to the 20-year baseline. At a 30-year horizon, the 10% spending change adds 90,000 relative to the unchanged 30-year case; the 10% income change adds 72,000. Together they add 162,000. In this invented household, modest changes to the monthly gap matter at least as much as changing the horizon. The calculation shows sensitivity, not the probability of any case.

To reproduce the table, multiply the monthly difference by 360 months for every 30-year row. For example, (2,750 − 1,800) × 360 = 342,000. Replace both monthly figures with your own after-tax or gross figures consistently. A lump sum should be modeled separately. If pensions change with inflation or a survivor loses benefits, split the horizon into stages and calculate each stage; a single fixed-gap total conceals those transitions.

My editorial takeaway is that a life-expectancy headline is a prompt to test the duration of a plan, not a shortcut to a required asset balance. Before comparing products, identify which input you can verify and which needs a range. This table cannot determine how much to invest or whether a household can sustain withdrawals.

What a modest real return does to the extra decade

The tables above ignore returns, which overstates the cost of a long retirement if savings keep earning something. I redid the baseline gap of 500 a month with a real return, meaning growth after inflation, and asked how much would have to be set aside on day one to cover the gap. The formula is the present value of 12 × years equal monthly payments, with the monthly rate set to (1 + annual real return)^(1/12) − 1. Payments are assumed to arrive at month-end. Units are arbitrary, and the 1% and 2% rates are test values rather than forecasts.

Lump sum needed at retirement to cover a 500-a-month gap (my own calculation)
Real return per year20 years30 years40 years
0%120,000180,000240,000
1%108,769155,555197,909
2%99,005135,606165,632

Two readings stand out. First, the extra decade is not a fixed price. Going from 30 to 40 years adds 60,000 at 0%, but only about 30,000 at 2%, because late payments are worth less in today's money. Second, the first 20 years account for 50% of the 40-year requirement at 0% and about 60% at 2%. A household that is short in the early years has little to gain from worrying only about age 95.

My own caution: the table flatters the long horizon, because it assumes the return arrives smoothly. A poor market in the first few years of withdrawals hurts far more than the same poor market later, and a simple average hides that. I read the 2% row as the optimistic edge of the range, and I would not plan around it without also looking at the 0% row.

Then change one assumption at a time

Spending

Housing costs can end, continue or rise. Irregular home repairs, care and medical expenses do not fit neatly into a fixed monthly average. Model them separately rather than presenting a falsely precise single figure.

Income

A public benefit estimate can depend on work history and claiming choices. Other recurring income may end when work stops or after a partner dies. Compare household spending with household income and test the survivor case if relevant.

Purchasing power and markets

A fixed amount of money may buy less over time. Returns on investments are uncertain, especially when withdrawals begin during a market decline. Testing lower returns or a higher cost of living reveals sensitivity; it does not predict either outcome.

Build a plan around jobs, not products

  1. Near-term access: keep money for foreseeable bills and unexpected costs available on appropriate terms.
  2. Essential income: identify which expenses must be covered regardless of market conditions and what reliable income is available.
  3. Longer horizons: test several retirement lengths and review assumptions as circumstances change.
  4. Contracts and providers: if considering an annuity or any investment, read fees, payout terms, risks and local protections before committing.
