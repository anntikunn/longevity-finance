THE DATA BEHIND THE QUESTION

How long might retirement last? Start with the right statistic.

Life expectancy at birth is widely quoted, but retirement planning begins much later. The more relevant population measure is remaining life expectancy at age 65: the average years remaining for people who have already reached 65, under the mortality rates used in the estimate.

Japan in international context

The OECD's Pensions at a Glance 2025 compares remaining life expectancy at age 65 using 2024 mortality rates. The figures below all use the same measure; they are not forecasts of any one reader's lifespan.

Approximate ages are simply 65 plus the reported remaining years. The OECD describes women in Australia, France, Korea and Spain as having more than 23 years remaining at age 65. For men, the OECD average is 18.5 years, or age 83.5. These are different population averages; they do not describe eligibility for pensions or explain why countries differ.

Source: OECD, Pensions at a Glance 2025, “Life expectancy,” Figure 6.2 and key results (2024 estimates based on UN World Population Prospects 2024). Data checked 5 October 2026.

Why the age-65 measure matters

A statistic “at birth” averages over a whole population from birth. Someone who has reached 65 has already passed the earlier ages, so the remaining-years measure answers a different question. Neither statistic is a retirement deadline. An average includes people who die earlier and people who live longer; a plan that ends exactly at the average leaves the longer-life case unexamined.

The OECD figures above are period life expectancies: they apply the mortality rates observed or estimated for different ages in a particular year. They do not incorporate every possible future improvement for a particular birth cohort. They also cannot account for an individual's health, sex-specific circumstances, household structure or finances.

A reproducible comparison: what changes between the 2024 and 2065 estimates?

The same OECD chapter also projects remaining years at 65 in 2065. For women in Japan, the period estimate rises from 24.9 in 2024 to 29.2 in 2065. Subtracting the two published figures gives 4.3 additional years; at the OECD average, 21.6 in 2024 plus the OECD's stated 3.7-year projected increase gives 25.3 in 2065. The Japan-versus-OECD gap is therefore 3.3 years in 2024 and 3.9 years in these 2065 period estimates. This comparison is our arithmetic from the OECD figures, not an OECD forecast for a particular reader.

Worked translation, not a savings target: If an illustrative household had a constant 500-unit monthly income gap, extending a hypothetical period by 4.3 years would add 500 × 12 × 4.3 = 25,800 units of cumulative gap. That exercise holds every other variable fixed. It does not say a Japanese woman aged 65 today should plan for exactly 29.2 years: the 2065 period estimate concerns mortality rates projected for 2065, and the OECD distinguishes period from cohort life expectancy. It also says nothing about a particular person's spending or survival probability.

My conclusion is narrower than the tempting headline “people will need 4.3 more years of savings.” A long horizon is worth testing, but the material unknowns for a household are its actual income, care costs, partner's circumstances and changing expenses. The scenario worksheet shows how a modest spending or income change can move the total as well as time does.

Connect the statistic to money without overclaiming

Suppose monthly spending exceeds dependable income by 500 units of your currency. An additional five years would mean 500 × 12 × 5 = 30,000 more units in a deliberately simplified calculation. That arithmetic illustrates why the planning horizon matters. It is not an amount everyone must save: spending, benefits, purchasing power, investment outcomes and care costs can change.

Use a range of planning horizons, such as the 20-, 30- and 40-year examples, then enter your own assumptions in the cash-flow calculator. Do not set the end of the plan by adding an average to your current age. If planning for a couple, also consider how income and expenses change after one partner dies.

Interpretation limit: This page compares population statistics, not personal probabilities. Country-level differences do not establish that any individual will live to the reported age, and they are not a basis for selecting a financial product. Pension rules and insurance protections vary by country.

What we will update
