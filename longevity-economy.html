MARKET STRUCTURE

An ageing population creates needs, not automatic profits

The longevity economy is broader than biotech. It includes housing, care, work, finance and technologies that help people remain independent. A demographic trend is only the first step in an investment analysis.

By Baruka · Reviewed 5 October 2026 · General education, not personal financial or medical advice.

Begin with a demographic fact, then stop extrapolating

OECD data project that the number of people aged 65 or older per 100 people aged 20–64 across the OECD will rise from 32.6 in 2024 to 55.2 in 2054. This old-age to working-age ratio describes population structure, not consumers' purchasing power or a company's revenue. A greater share of older residents may raise public costs as well as private demand.

Nor does every country follow the same path. The OECD highlights particularly rapid ageing in Korea, while national pension and care systems differ. Japan illustrates why the topic matters for a .jp publication, but local rules, family structures and payment channels still determine what a business can sell. A single worldwide “longevity market” number hides those differences.

Baruka's lens: I draw a line from a documented need to a named payer before discussing an investable company. A demographic chart is the opening question, not the answer.

Three demographic paths, one commercial question

Using the same OECD definition—people aged 65 and over per 100 people aged 20–64—Japan moves from 54.9 in 2024 to a projected 80.0 in 2054, Korea from 29.3 to 84.5, and the OECD average from 32.6 to 55.2. The figures come from the OECD table linked below. The changes in ratio points are 25.1, 55.2 and 22.6 respectively. Korea's rise is steepest of these three, but Japan starts much higher. Neither comparison measures the number of paying customers.

The denominator matters: this ratio can rise when the working-age population shrinks, even if a particular service's customer base does not grow at the same pace. It is also a population ratio, not the share of a company’s revenue earned from older customers. An investor who multiplies the projected ratio by today's sales growth is mixing unlike quantities. These differences are calculated from the OECD's figures, and the commercial reading is my inference.

For a hypothetical home-adaptation provider, I would look for three separate pieces of evidence: eligible homes in its service area, the fraction of households or payers actually ordering installations, and contribution after installation and customer acquisition costs. A demographic projection supplies none of those three. If orders rise but installation capacity is constrained, revenue may grow more slowly than demand. If revenue rises but labor costs rise faster, the market opportunity may not reach shareholders.

The rows are an editorial framework, not an OECD estimate of market size. A beneficiary can value a service while lacking the ability or legal permission to pay for it. In a regulated market, the party approving a service may differ from the person using it. That gap can make a promising product commercially slow.

Demand is not a margin

Suppose a home service reduces avoidable disruption for customers. Revenue still depends on how many eligible households can be reached and retained, the price they can bear, and the cost of providing reliable staff. If wages or acquisition costs rise faster than price, an expanding service can remain unprofitable. An investor should inspect actual unit economics rather than multiply the number of older residents by an arbitrary annual fee.

The same logic applies to diagnostics. A test might detect a risk, but a payer may require evidence that using the test changes outcomes or lowers costs. A product's relevance to healthy ageing is not the same as its clinical approval, reimbursement status or shareholder return. These distinctions are particularly important when a company pitches several industries at once.

Three scenarios for an ageing-related business

  1. Useful and paid for: The service demonstrates benefits, identifies a payer and earns enough after delivery and acquisition costs. The investment question then becomes valuation and competition.
  2. Useful but difficult to monetize: Benefits appear real, but the payer is unclear or approval takes longer than the company's funding permits. Customers may still gain while shareholders do not.
  3. Compelling story with weak evidence: The company markets a large older population without showing adoption, outcomes or financial durability. In that case demographic growth does not repair the missing evidence.

This scenario method is a way to make the thesis falsifiable. Decide in advance which observation would move a company from one case to another. Compare competitors and substitutes, including non-technology changes such as housing design or ordinary public services. Revisit data rather than repeating a market-size headline.

Keep the household side visible

Longevity finance is also about funding one's own long life. A household does not need to profit from the ageing economy to benefit from a clearer spending plan. Start with recurring expenses and income, examine the longer-life scenarios, and only then decide what, if any, role specialized investments can have. Stocks exposed to older customers may move in the same market downturn that affects a retiree's withdrawal portfolio.

My editorial aim is to connect population change, service design and cash flow while making uncertainty visible. That is more informative than calling every healthcare business a “longevity winner.” It is not a forecast of any country's policy or any security's return.
